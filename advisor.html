How four GSSC services run today

How I would compare four UNICEF shared services with other organisations

Service Design Advisor, UNICEF Global Shared Services Centre (GSSC)

What I would do in 2027

I would compare four GSSC services (recruitment, consultant contracts, travel and offboarding) with the same services in other organisations. For each service, I would write a brief and then join its workshop in Budapest. In this proposal, clients are the offices, staff and consultants who request a service, and a service team is the GSSC team that runs it.

Finding and comparing the steps where clients get stuck

The Service Design Investment Project Manager, another consultant, would run a workshop for each service. Before each one, I would work with the service team to find the steps where clients get stuck, such as where they wait for an answer or get a form sent back. I would ask to join the interviews the Investment Project Manager runs, so nobody is asked the same questions twice.

  • To find these steps, I would ask the staff who answer client questions, read the request records and talk to clients.
  • I would rank the steps by how many clients get stuck at each, for how long, and how hard each is to fix.
  • For the top steps, I would look first at UN organisations with the same problem, since I expect many of the same rules to apply. I would start with the organisations that work with UNICEF in the UN Business Innovations Group to grow shared services across the UN. They include the UN Development Programme (UNDP), the World Food Programme, the UN Refugee Agency, the UN Office for Project Services, the Food and Agriculture Organization, the World Health Organization and the UN Secretariat. Outside the UN, I would talk to people who run shared services and to companies that provide them.
  • In each organisation, I would study how staff handle the step and how they check whether a change fixed it.
  • Before I put a practice in a brief, I would test it with the service team, since another organisation may rely on rules or staff that UNICEF doesn't have.

Where I would start in each service

  • In recruitment, the UN Joint Inspection Unit wrote in 2026 that people it interviewed reported delays before vacancies are posted, and that this stage "is not yet systematically monitored". I would start with that stage. The UNDP shared services centre reported that in 2025, 883 of its 1,762 recruitment cases were "completed within 70 calendar days". I would ask its staff how they measure this.
  • For consultant contracts, the GSSC tells consultants on its website that an invoice sent before their supervisor has accepted and approved the deliverables "will be rejected". The consultant would then have to send it again. I would start with that rule, and study how other organisations that pay per deliverable approve the work and pay for it.
  • In travel, clients send their requests to the GSSC through a system called the Service Gateway. In 2026, the GSSC advertised a travel improvement role. The person hired would study work done "within and outside the Service Gateway" and find "inconsistencies, duplication, outdated information" in travel guidance. I would ask other UN travel teams whether they keep all requests in one system, and how they keep their guidance up to date.
  • In offboarding, a staff member who leaves gets final pay from Payroll and Offboarding, separation travel from the Travel Hub and a pension from the UN Joint Staff Pension Fund, and may have to contact each of them separately. The Fund says it pays within 15 business days once it has all the papers, and lists a late separation notice from the employer as "out of the Fund's control". Every UN employer sends this notice to the same Fund, so I would compare how quickly different employers send it. I would start with that notice.

The eight deliverables in 2027

The plan and the starting figures

From about 18 January, I would agree with each service team and the Investment Project Manager on which steps to study and which organisations to compare with. In the framework, I would list the steps and measures I would compare, and where each figure comes from. In the baseline, I would record the starting figures, such as how many clients get stuck at each step and for how long. I would begin with the 96.5% average client satisfaction and 92.5% service level compliance that the UNICEF GSSC reported for the whole centre in 2025. I found no published UNICEF figures for single services, so I would also use the records each service team keeps.

Deliverable 1, the inception and benchmarking plan, is due 15 February. Deliverable 2, the framework and baseline, is due 26 February.

A brief for each service

For each service, I would write a short slide deck on how other organisations handle the chosen steps, and compare the changes the service team plans with what those organisations did. I would send it to the service team two weeks before their workshop, so they can read it first. At the workshop in Budapest, I would test the ideas the team proposes against the practices in the brief.

Deliverables 3 to 6, the four briefs, are due 15 April, 28 May, 5 July and 20 August.

The benchmark report

I would combine the four briefs into one benchmark report and prepare it as a presentation for senior leaders. In it, I would check each change the service teams plan against evidence from other organisations, and set out which practices from those organisations UNICEF could use, what could go wrong and what UNICEF would gain.

Deliverable 7, the benchmark report, is due 15 September.

Review of the four implementation plans

For each service, I would review the proposed requirements, designs, roadmaps and project plans against the benchmark report. For each change, I would advise whether it could be done, in what order, whether it would last and what benefits to expect.

Deliverable 8, the review with final recommendations, is due 6 December.

Risks and what I would do

  • The first two deliverables need 30 days of work in the 30 weekdays between about 18 January and 26 February, so I would have no spare days if a team were slow to send its records. I would ask each service team for its records in the first week, while I write the inception plan.
  • Other organisations may share only what they publish. I would start with their annual reports and ask for more through the UN Business Innovations Group and professional networks.
  • Organisations may count different things under the same name. The UNDP centre reported an overall recruitment turnaround time of 83 calendar days for its 1,762 cases in 2025. In the same report, it gave a 101-day average for 2,064 requests from UNDP and partner entities. Before I compare a figure, I would ask staff at the other organisation what they count, and agree with the service team how to count the UNICEF cases the same way.
